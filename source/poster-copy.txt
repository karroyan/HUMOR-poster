Why compare within a template?
A global “funniness score” assumes that unrelated jokes share one scale. We
instead learn relative quality inside a coherent group G, usually one template.
Fig. 4. Absolute VLM scores (left) poorly separate real memes from blank images;
group-wise ranks (right) provide clearer distinctions. Lower rank is better.
Independent check: 9 annotators, 5 groups, 30 memes. Gemini–human
agreement: Spearman 0.72; Kendall τ = 0.63.
Data: 3,345 train / 368 test instances. Teacher: Doubao-1.5-Vision-Pro. Human
ratings and MaxDiff rankings are separate from constructed training pairs.

Learn preferences, not global scores
Training pairs share a template. Five constructed difficulty tiers teach a VLM reward model to distinguish
disrupted meaning, weak humor and plausible alternatives.

harder model-written alternatives
Pairwise probability
p(i > j | G) = σ[s(i) − s(j)]
Expected Borda Count
EBC(i) = Σ p(i > j | G),  j ≠ i
Binary cross-entropy trains the pairwise comparator. Expected wins induce a group-local preference
distribution q. The policy learns to favor higher-ranked captions while staying near its CoT reference.
Policy loss = −E[Σ q log π(c | I)] + β E[KL(π || πref)]

What the analysis guarantees
Exploration
E[h] ≥ 1 − (1 − α)δ
If promising paths retain mass α and the remaining quality gap is at most δ, expected normalized humor has this lower bound. More paths
alone do not establish the assumptions.
KL stability
H(π) ≥ H(ref) − √(ΔKL / 2)
For bounded humor h in [0,1], KL limits possible degradation. Preference consistency additionally assumes a monotone observation
model; neither result guarantees monotonic gains.
